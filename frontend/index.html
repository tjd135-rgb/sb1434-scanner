The Bill · SB 1434 (§163.2525, Florida Statutes)

The statutory briefing. For scanner methodology and technical detail, see How It Works. For practical usage of the tool, see the Scanner Guide.

1 What is SB 1434?

SB 1434 is Florida's Infill Redevelopment Act, codified at §163.2525, F.S. The bill creates a streamlined pathway for redeveloping underutilized parcels sitting on environmentally impacted land.

The problem it solves

South Florida has thousands of acres of contamination-adjacent land — old gas stations, industrial sites, dry-cleaner corridors, brownfields — sitting idle because cleanup costs and regulatory uncertainty make redevelopment uneconomical. Meanwhile the region faces a housing and commercial supply shortage. SB 1434 is a targeted incentive to bring those parcels back into productive use.

How the mechanism works

Parcels that qualify get access to streamlined permitting, potential density bonuses, and reduced regulatory barriers for infill redevelopment. The exact bundle of unlocks depends on the pathway (see §3 below) — a not-ringed golf course is treated very differently from an industrial site inside a brownfield area.

Who benefits

  • Developers who can pair environmental remediation with new construction get a cost/entitlement edge that made deals uneconomic without the unlock.
  • Municipalities get blighted or contaminated land converted to tax-generating use — infrastructure served but under-taxed for decades.
  • Communities get investment in neglected neighborhoods, often around long-standing environmental eyesores.

Geographic scope

Only Miami-Dade, Broward, and Palm Beach — the three counties in Florida meeting the statute's population and municipality thresholds.

See §6 Related Florida statutes below for how SB 1434 relates to the Live Local Act, Bert Harris, and the FDEP Brownfield Program.

2 The five qualification gates

A parcel must pass all five to appear on the scanner. Each gate covers a specific piece of the statute.

Gate 1 · Minimum 5 acres

The test: LND_SQFOOT / 43,560 ≥ 5.
Why the statute has it: SB 1434 targets meaningful redevelopment sites — infill assemblage of individual lots isn't what the bill was written for.
Data: DOR NAL land square footage.
Edge case: Parcels at exactly 5.0 acres are borderline — DOR's LND_SQFOOT can round; verify against the county PA record before committing.

Gate 2 · Tri-county location

The test: county_fips ∈ {23 Miami-Dade, 16 Broward, 60 Palm Beach}.
Why: The statute was drafted for South Florida's specific environmental and development context.
Data: DOR NAL CO_NO field.

Gate 3 · Environmental trigger (either/or)

The heart of SB 1434 — this is the gate that makes it different from every other density unlock. Two qualifying paths, either is sufficient:

  • Trigger B: Inside an FDEP brownfield area. These are formal cleanup zones designated by FDEP under §376.80. Being inside one of these polygons is the cleanest possible qualification signal.
  • Trigger A: Within 1,500 ft of a DEP cleanup site. Individual contamination points from the DEP Contamination Locator Map — gas stations, dry cleaners, industrial spills, petroleum leaks. Proximity, not containment.

Data: 624 FDEP brownfield-area polygons + 2,363 DEP cleanup-site points (tri-county subset of a ~10k statewide dataset).

Gate 4 · Residential adjacency

The test: at least one parcel with DOR use code 001-009 (residential) within 500 ft.
Why: the statute requires the site to be near existing residential so the infill serves an established community — not a greenfield play.
Data: DOR NAL use codes on all neighboring parcels.
Caveat: DOR use codes don't capture every residential use — condo sub-units, mixed-use residential, etc. If the scanner flags a parcel as "no adjacency detected", verify via aerial before dismissing it.

Gate 5 · Exclusions

Certain land uses are explicitly protected from this redevelopment path.

  • Agricultural (DOR 050-069). Excluded outright.
  • Government-owned public parks (DOR 082 + govt owner name).
  • Military buffer — within ¼ mile (1,320 ft) of any installation listed in §163.3175(2). Applies via ST_DWithin on installation polygons.
  • Institutional (DOR 070-079) — schools, churches, hospitals, cultural orgs. Not actionable as redevelopment targets at scale.
  • Utility (DOR 091-097 or utility-company owner). The 15-year title lookback the statute requires isn't automatable, so these are excluded rather than flagged.
  • Broad government & conservation ownership — any owner name containing COUNTY (with or without OF), CITY OF / TOWN OF / VILLAGE OF, MUNICIPAL, GOVERNMENT, DISTRICT, STATE OF, UNITED STATES, SCHOOL BOARD, WATER MANAGEMENT / WATER MGMT / SOUTH FLORIDA WATER / SOUTH FLA WATER (all common abbreviations), DEPARTMENT OF / DEPT OF, AUTHORITY, HOUSING AUTH, PORT AUTH, plus land-conservation entities (CONSERVATION, PRESERVE, LAND TRUST, NATURE) and CRA / REDEVELOPMENT AGENCY / TAXING. Excluded unless the DOR use code is commercial or industrial (010-049) AND the owner-name doesn't flag transportation infrastructure (AIRPORT / AIR PORT / TRANSIT / BUS TERMINAL). None of these owners are realistic sellers.
  • No DOR use code. Parcels with NULL or blank dor_uc can't be classified into a property type and are excluded outright — they're typically data-quality issues in the DOR file, not actionable parcels.
  • Military owner patterns — parcels whose owner name contains AIR BASE / AIR FORCE / MILITARY / NAVY / ARMY / NATIONAL GUARD / COAST GUARD, catching cases that geometric buffering missed.
3 What the bill unlocks — density, height, and value by property type

The scanner organizes deals by property type (what the parcel is today) — but the value each type represents depends on the SB 1434 pathway the parcel maps to. The pathway is how the parcel qualifies under the statute; it shows in every parcel's detail drawer beneath the property-type header.

What SB 1434 actually unlocks vs. what local code controls. SB 1434 does not itself set a specific FAR, height limit, or units-per-acre cap. Instead it does three things: (1) streamlines permitting for redevelopment of environmentally impacted parcels, (2) grants qualifying parcels access to whatever density and height the underlying comprehensive plan allows — without the usual downzoning or NIMBY delay tools municipalities often deploy, and (3) for ringed golf courses only, imposes a compatibility overlay that caps density around 4-8 units/acre to protect the surrounding single-family neighborhood.

Building height, FAR, unit count, and setbacks are still set by the local comp plan and zoning code. The typical Florida infill densities cited below are what the underlying zoning most commonly allows in tri-county South Florida — they are not statutory guarantees. Every deal needs a local zoning read.

Below, each property-type bucket is paired with the specific SB 1434 unlock available. The scanner also computes a rough max-units estimate per parcel (acres × typical units/acre) surfaced in the parcel drawer and filterable via Min est. units.

Golf courses — not ringed (Pathway 2) ★

The highest-value unlock in the entire dataset. When a golf course is not surrounded by single-family residential on 80%+ of its perimeter, no SB 1434 density overlay applies. The developer can build at whatever density the underlying zoning allows, potentially converting 100+ acres of low-value recreational land into high-density mixed-use or residential.

Typical unlock: 25-35 units/acre on the buildable portion (depending on the underlying comp plan — Miami-Dade T5 / T6 zones support 45-60 u/ac, suburban Broward and PBC typically 15-25). Building heights typically 4-8 stories under compatible zoning, taller in urban cores. Cleanup obligation is usually minimal (fertilizer / pesticide surface remediation, occasionally lead from range decks). This is why Pathway 2 golf courses are the scanner's "top deals" and get the gold star — the scanner defaults a rough 30 u/ac estimate.

Golf courses — ringed or partial (Pathways 1 / 1B)

When a course IS ringed by SF residential (80%+ perimeter), the SB 1434 overlay applies and caps density to be compatible with the surrounding neighborhood. Still developable, but the upside is capped — plays here are limited to repositioning (banquet, wellness, event, resort) rather than full residential conversion.

Typical unlock: 3-8 units/acre max, often single- family or low-rise attached at densities comparable to the surrounding neighborhood. Building heights capped at 2-3 stories to preserve compatibility. Partially ringed courses (40-79%) are borderline; the classification depends on how you measure the perimeter, so a detailed boundary survey can move a course from 1B to 2 in your underwriting. The scanner uses a rough 5 u/ac estimate for ringed golf.

Industrial / Warehouse (Pathway 3)

Often the largest footprints (50-500+ acres) on the most contaminated land. DOR 040-049 — warehouses, light manufacturing, packing plants, open storage. The value unlock is converting obsolete industrial into residential, mixed-use, or logistics. Cleanup costs are real but can be structured with FDEP Voluntary Cleanup Tax Credits (VCTC) and brownfield site rehabilitation agreements.

Typical unlock: 30-50 units/acre on the redeveloped portion in urban cores (Wynwood, Hialeah corridors), 15-30 in suburban industrial (Doral, Sunrise). Heights commonly 6-15 stories under mixed-use overlays; industrial land in Opportunity Zones frequently supports 8-12 stories. Scanner default: 40 u/ac. Zoning-change risk is real: neighborhoods that grew up around industrial often organize hard against conversion.

Commercial / Retail (Pathway 4)

Aging strip centers, big-box stores, and regional malls on brownfield-adjacent land. SB 1434 can enable densification beyond what current commercial zoning allows — turning single-story retail into mixed-use vertical development.

Typical unlock: 25-40 units/acre with ground-floor retail preserved. Heights 4-10 stories depending on the corridor. Scanner default: 30 u/ac. Verify tenant tails (a long-dated NNN kills the teardown timing) and check shopping-center reciprocal-easement agreements, which can lock density for decades.

Office (Pathway 5)

DOR 017-019. Underperforming suburban office parks. The play is conversion to residential or mixed-use, enabled by the streamlined permitting pathway.

Typical unlock: 25-35 units/acre for suburban garden-office redevelopment; 40-70 u/ac in Miami CBD tower-conversion cases. Heights typically 5-12 stories, higher for tower conversions. Scanner default: 30 u/ac. Post-COVID vacancy + SB 1434 unlock = teardown-to-rental. Medical office (018) has stickier tenants than plain professional (017).

Residential redevelopment (Pathway 7)

DOR 001-009 on 5+ acres — mobile-home parks and aging garden apartments.

Typical unlock: often a 3-4× density multiplier vs. existing. Mobile-home parks at 8-12 u/ac can redevelop to 25-40 u/ac; garden apartments at 15-20 u/ac to 40-60 u/ac. Heights typically 4-8 stories. Scanner default: 25 u/ac. Mind Florida tenant-displacement statutes — mobile-home-park closures require 6+ months notice plus relocation assistance.

Auto / Fuel (Pathway 9)

DOR 025-028 + 048. Gas stations and auto-repair on contaminated sites. Small footprints but high environmental-trigger certainty — underground storage tanks are well-documented.

Typical unlock: 30-60 units/acre — small parcels (¼ to 2 acres typical) support intense vertical infill when the underlying corridor zoning allows. Heights 5-12 stories in the right corridor. Scanner default: 40 u/ac. Cleanup cost IS the deal — expect Phase II ESA and a full remediation estimate before LOI.

Hospitality (Pathway 10)

DOR 039. Aging hotels/motels — often in prime locations near beaches or downtown areas.

Typical unlock: 40-70 units/acre for hotel-to- condo or mixed-use conversion (South Beach, Downtown Miami). Heights 8-25 stories in the right locations. Scanner default: 45 u/ac. On-site historic dry-cleaner exposure is common, plus soft ADR at older properties makes teardown competitive with reposition. Union or brand constraints can complicate exit.

Vacant commercial (Pathway 11)

DOR 010. Empty commercial land in cleanup zones — no demolition cost, no existing tenants. Pure development play.

Typical unlock: 25-40 units/acre depending on the corridor; heights match the surrounding built context. Scanner default: 30 u/ac. But vacant commercial in a brownfield zone often means the last owner walked away because remediation was uneconomic; read the FDEP file before pursuing.

Mixed use (Pathway 12)

DOR 030-031. Already mixed-use, eligible for intensification under SB 1434's streamlined pathway.

Typical unlock: 30-50 units/acre — the intensification vs. what's already built. Heights typically match the existing FAR envelope + 20-40% under SB 1434 permitting. Scanner default: 35 u/ac. Check current FAR utilization vs. what SB 1434 unlocks — sometimes the existing envelope already burns the entitlement.

Restaurant / Entertainment & Other

Everything that passed all five gates but doesn't fit the buckets above. Scanner defaults: 25 u/ac (restaurant/entertainment), 15 u/ac (other). Eyeball the DOR use code and current use before deciding whether to pursue.

Building height under SB 1434

SB 1434 does not directly set height limits. However, the statute's streamlined-permitting provisions typically let qualifying parcels reach the maximum height allowed by the underlying zoning without discretionary review, which in practice means:

  • Miami-Dade urban core (T5-T6): up to 25-40+ stories in Downtown / Brickell / Wynwood / Edgewater where zoning permits.
  • Broward & PBC commercial corridors: typically 6-15 stories along US-1, Federal Highway, Sunrise Boulevard.
  • Suburban Broward / PBC: generally 4-8 stories.
  • Ringed golf courses: 2-3 stories (overlay-constrained).
  • Coastal beachfront (South Beach, Fort Lauderdale Beach): subject to CBRS / hurricane-code and local height caps that can override.

Bottom line: SB 1434 removes the permitting friction around reaching the underlying zoning's density and height caps. It does not raise those caps. Every deal needs a specific comp-plan + zoning read on the parcel.

4 Environmental triggers explained

Brownfield area (Trigger B)

An FDEP-designated zone where cleanup is planned or under way under §376.80. These are formally recognized by the state, cover a substantial footprint (typically dozens to hundreds of acres each), and are usually driven by a local government initiative. If your parcel's centroid is inside one of these polygons, Trigger B is satisfied — this is the cleanest qualification signal because the state has already acknowledged the contamination and the community has bought into cleanup.

Cleanup site (Trigger A)

An individual contamination point from the DEP Contamination Locator Map. Gas stations, dry cleaners, industrial spills, petroleum leaks, small chemical incidents. These aren't zones — they're specific pinpoints. A parcel qualifies if its centroid is within 1,500 ft of any one of these points (proximity, not containment). The 1,500 ft radius is the statute's test — it's meant to capture parcels that are meaningfully affected by an adjacent contamination event even if the parcel itself isn't the source.

Both

Some parcels sit inside a brownfield area and near a cleanup site — the belt-and-suspenders qualification. Common for old industrial corridors where the state designated a brownfield area because of the individual cleanup points inside it.

The scanner flags presence, not severity

Neither trigger tells you how bad the contamination is or how expensive cleanup will be. A parcel near a former dry cleaner might have $50k of PCE plume remediation, or it might have $5M of soil-vapor abatement. Every SB 1434 deal requires contamination-severity diligence — pull the FDEP file, commission a Phase I ESA, budget for a Phase II if warranted.

Why the statute uses two triggers

Brownfield areas are the state's formal recognition mechanism, but designations lag. A newly-discovered spill might not get an area designation for years. Trigger A (proximity to individual cleanup sites) closes that gap and lets the statute reach parcels that are affected in practice even without formal zone designation.

5 The golf-course ring test

Why golf courses get special treatment. Golf courses in South Florida were platted decades ago inside (or as the anchor of) single-family subdivisions. Converting a ringed golf course to housing fundamentally changes the character of the residential neighborhood that grew up around it, so the statute imposes an overlay that caps redevelopment density on ringed courses. Un-ringed courses (open on multiple sides, or adjacent to commercial/industrial) don't create the same character problem, so the overlay doesn't apply and full density flexibility is available.

Classification thresholds:

  • ≥ 80% ringed → overlay applies (Pathway 1).
  • 40-79% partial → overlay likely applies, shape-dependent (Pathway 1B). Order a boundary survey.
  • < 40% not ringed → overlay does NOT apply — the top-deal case (Pathway 2).

For the technical detail of how the 12-sample perimeter algorithm actually runs, see §4 The ring test algorithm in the How It Works tab.

6 Related Florida statutes

SB 1434 is distinct from — though sometimes complementary with — several other Florida land-use statutes.

FDEP Brownfield Program (§376.77-376.86)

The broader statutory framework that creates FDEP brownfield areas in the first place. SB 1434 uses those designations as one of its Gate 3 triggers, so the two statutes are tightly linked. Brownfield-area designation typically requires a local-government initiative and a formal Brownfield Site Rehabilitation Agreement (BSRA) with FDEP.

Live Local Act (SB 102 / CS/SB 328)

LLA unlocks residential density on commercial/industrial land for attainable housing, regardless of environmental status. Some SB 1434 parcels also qualify under LLA, but the triggers are different — LLA cares about housing affordability commitments; SB 1434 cares about environmental impact. When both apply, developers often stack the two unlocks.

Bert Harris Act (§70.001, F.S.)

Property-rights / compensation statute. Unrelated mechanism but occasionally invoked when SB 1434 unlocks butt against local downzoning — if a municipality attempts to restrict a parcel's SB 1434 unlock via a comp-plan amendment, the owner may have a Bert Harris claim for the taken value.

Local comprehensive-plan amendments

SB 1434 unlocks statutory rights, but density is ultimately governed by the local comprehensive plan and zoning code. In practice, developers pair the SB 1434 qualification with a comp-plan amendment (or FLUM change) to actually deliver the density. Municipalities differ on whether they treat SB 1434 as an entitlement or a bargaining starting point.

How It Works · Scanner Methodology

The technical detail behind the scanner — data sources, screening logic, property-type classification, ring-test algorithm, and known limitations. For the statute itself, see The Bill. For practical usage, see the Scanner Guide.

1 Data pipeline

The scanner joins six independent datasets into a single screening pass. Each is refreshed on its own cadence.

  • DOR NAL parcels — Florida DOR Name-Address-Legal file, 2025 vintage. 2,342,443 parcels loaded across the three counties. Annual refresh (Q3 publish).
  • Parcel centroids — FL Statewide Parcel Centroid FeatureServer (Esri Living Atlas). Joined to NAL via zero-padded parcel_id per county. Coverage varies (~62% Miami-Dade, higher elsewhere — see Limitations below).
  • FDEP Brownfield Areas — DEP OpenData Layer 0. 624 designated area polygons statewide.
  • DEP Cleanup Sites — DEP Contamination Locator Map, Environment MapServer Layer 1. 2,363 tri-county points filtered from a ~10k-statewide dataset.
  • Miami-Dade UDB — Miami-Dade County GIS export. Polygonized on ingest from the county's LineString publication (§4B below).
  • Military installations — six tri-county installations per §163.3175(2), from a hardcoded reference list. Point-buffered footprints; ¼-mile exclusion computed at screen time.

The scanner is only as fresh as the last run. After a NAL or brownfield/cleanup refresh, hit POST /admin/run-screening to rebuild qualifying_parcels, then POST /admin/run-ring-test to reclassify golf.

2 How screening works

Screening is a single INSERT ... SELECT in PostGIS that materializes the qualifying_parcels table from a full sweep of the 2.34M parcels. Every gate is enforced in the WHERE clause; parcels that survive all five gates are inserted with their pathway, env-trigger source, and value context copied from the parcels row.

Gate-by-gate implementation

  • Gate 1 (5+ acres): p.lnd_sqfoot >= 217800.
  • Gate 2 (Tri-county): p.county_fips IN ('23','16','60').
  • Gate 3 (Environmental trigger): two LEFT JOIN LATERAL … LIMIT 1 subqueries against brownfield_areas (containment via ST_Contains) and cleanup_sites (proximity via ST_DWithin(..., 457.2 m)). Row survives if EITHER subquery returned. env_trigger records which — brownfield_area, cleanup_site, or both.
  • Gate 4 (Residential adjacency): updated in a separate post-INSERT pass rather than the main WHERE — running an EXISTS+ ST_DWithin check against the full 2.34M parcels for every candidate row would 100× the runtime. The follow-up UPDATE runs only against the ~2k rows that already passed Gates 1-3 and 5.
  • Gate 5 (Exclusions): a chain of NOT conditions in the WHERE, one per exclusion category (agricultural / govt park / military buffer / institutional / utility / airport / broad govt-with-transport-carve-out).

A parcel is "qualifying" if it survives all five gates. Everything on the map/list is qualifying by construction — the scanner has no unqualifying rows to show you.

4 The ring-test algorithm

For each qualifying golf-course parcel (DOR 038):

  1. Approximate the parcel boundary as a circle of equivalent area: r = √(acres · 4046.86 / π). We have the centroid and acreage, not the actual polygon.
  2. Compute 12 sample bearings, one every 30°, at a distance of r + 200 ft from the centroid — a ring 200 ft outside the approximated boundary. Uses PostGIS ST_Project(..., geography, distance, azimuth).
  3. For each sample point, KNN-look up the nearest other parcel (excluding the golf parcel itself) via geom <-> sample_pt ordered by distance, LIMIT 1.
  4. Check whether that nearest parcel has a DOR use code in 001-009 (single-family residential). Count SF-residential hits.
  5. Divide hits by 12 for the perimeter percentage. Bucket: ≥80% → ringed, 40-79% → partially_ringed, <40% → not_ringed.

Per-bearing samples (bearing angle, nearest parcel, DOR code, is-residential) are archived in the parcel's ring_test_samples JSONB column so downstream UI can visualize which side of a course is the un-ringed edge.

5 Known limitations
  • Centroid-based analysis. Every spatial check uses the parcel's DOR centroid — not its actual polygon. On a long/narrow or L-shaped parcel, the centroid can misrepresent Gate 3 containment or the ring-test perimeter. Always verify borderline cases via aerial.
  • Miami-Dade centroid coverage ~62%. The FL Statewide Centroid FeatureServer omits condo sub-units in Miami-Dade — condo folios have parent-lot centroids only. Non-condo Miami-Dade coverage is 99%+.
  • UDB boundary is polygonized from lines. Miami-Dade publishes the UDB as a LineString collection. We build a polygon at ingest by ST_Polygonize(ST_Union(g)) and take the largest ring. Result is close-but-not-authoritative; parcels within ~50 ft of the boundary should be eyeballed against the county's canonical map.
  • Gate 4 false negatives. DOR use codes don't capture every residential use — condo sub-units, mixed-use ground-floor retail with residential above, etc. If the scanner flags a parcel as "no adjacency detected", verify via aerial before dismissing it.
  • Contamination severity not available. Gate 3 flags presence of an environmental trigger. A parcel near a former dry cleaner might have $50k of PCE plume remediation, or $5M of soil-vapor abatement — the scanner can't tell you which. Pull the FDEP file and commission a Phase I ESA for every deal.
  • No zoning / entitlement data. DOR use codes are what the assessor thinks the parcel is used for, not what the local zoning allows. Actual buildable density requires a local comp-plan + zoning code lookup — the scanner surfaces qualifying parcels; it can't tell you the deal is entitleable.
  • Data recency. NAL is annual (Q3); FDEP brownfield / cleanup layers are updated ad-hoc. The scanner runs against whatever was last ingested — see §1 for the refresh commands.
6 Field glossary
Parcel ID
DOR-assigned unique identifier (folio in Miami-Dade, PCN in Palm Beach). Zero-padded to the county's canonical length.
DOR Use Code
Florida DOR 3-digit land-use classification. Reference: DOR Use Codes PDF.
Just Value (JV)
DOR-assessed total market value including improvements. Not sale price — but a decent proxy for scale.
Land Value
DOR-assessed value of the land component only. Compare to JV to derive the land/improvement ratio.
Land/improvement ratio
lnd_val / (jv − lnd_val). Ratio ≥ 1 means the land is worth as much or more than what's built on it — the classic underutilized-parcel signal. Ratio 999 = vacant / near-vacant.
Acres
LND_SQFOOT / 43,560. Land area, not building footprint.
Property Type
Client-side bucket derived from DOR use code. What the parcel actually is today. See §3.
SB 1434 Pathway
Server-side classification of how the parcel qualifies under the statute — one of 12 stable pathways plus pathway_golf_pending.
Env trigger
Which Gate 3 source qualified the parcel: brownfield_area, cleanup_site, or both.
UDB status
Miami-Dade only. inside / outside. Not an SB 1434 exclusion — a friction indicator.
Ring test result
Golf courses only. ringed / partially_ringed / not_ringed. See §4.
Adjacent residential
Whether any DOR 001-009 parcel exists within 500 ft. Passing Gate 4.

Scanner Guide · Using This Tool

Practical workflow, keyboard tips, and how to get the most out of every panel. For the statute itself see The Bill; for scanner methodology see How It Works.

1 Ten-minute workflow
  1. Note the default. On load the scanner shows only land-heavy parcels (Min land/imp ratio ≥ 1.0) — land worth as much or more than what's built on it, the classic underutilized-parcel signal. Clear the ratio input to see every qualifying parcel.
  2. Filter by Property Type. That's what the dropdown is — what the parcel actually is today (Warehouse, Golf Course, Shopping Center, Mobile Home Park). The map legend colors match. Property type is distinct from SB 1434 pathway — the pathway (how it qualifies) shows in the drawer under each parcel.
  3. Hit the gold Golf Opportunities tile when you want the top deals. It sets Property Type = Golf + Ring Test = not-ringed in one click.
  4. Narrow by county if you have a target market. Miami-Dade has the deepest inventory; Broward and Palm Beach are smaller pools but often cleaner titles.
  5. Click a marker to open the detail drawer. Skim the gate checklist, redev angle, and next steps. Every panel is scrollable independently — the map stays put.
  6. Toggle Satellite basemap when scanning a cluster. OSM hides parcel context; satellite shows what's actually there.
  7. Star parcels as you go. The star icon in each card and detail drawer adds the parcel to your watchlist (stored locally in your browser).
  8. Add short notes to your watchlisted parcels. The note field appears in the detail drawer once a parcel is starred.
  9. Toggle Watchlist in the top bar to filter the map/list to just your starred parcels.
  10. Download Watchlist as CSV when you're ready to review offline or share.
2 Reading the map

Marker color = property type (see legend, bottom-right of the map — greens for golf/residential, gray for industrial, blue for commercial, etc.).
Marker size = √acres, so a 400-acre course looks 4× the diameter of a 25-acre parcel.
Gold star markers are the not-ringed golf courses — 30% larger with a heavier outline so they pop. Ringed and partially-ringed golf courses stay green (the property-type color).

Floating legend is clickable — clicking any property-type row filters the map to that type; the golf-star row filters to not-ringed golf specifically.

Satellite toggle in the filter bar swaps OSM streets for Esri World Imagery. Streets give you addresses and street names; satellite shows parcel context — buildings, greenspace, adjacent uses.

Overlay toggles in the filter row: Brownfield areas loads all 624 FDEP polygons as a translucent gold overlay so you can see the qualifying zones at a glance; UDB boundary (MDC) draws the Miami-Dade Urban Development Boundary as a blue dashed outline. Both are lazy-loaded on first toggle and stay on the map as you pan/zoom.

3 Reading the detail drawer

Click any parcel (map marker or list card) to open the detail drawer on the right. Everything you need to underwrite the parcel is one scroll.

  • Property type (top): the actual current use from the DOR use code — Warehouse, Golf Course, Shopping Center, etc.
  • SB 1434 pathway (just below, color-matched): the redevelopment classification the scanner assigned.
  • Star icon: click to add to watchlist. Note field appears when starred.
  • Parcel ID chip + Copy: the ID you paste into the county property-appraiser search.
  • Value context: JV, land value, and land/improvement ratio.
  • Gate checklist: every one of the five statutory gates with a ✅ / ℹ️ / ⚠️ icon. Green = passed cleanly, blue = data-quality note (verify manually), yellow = borderline.
  • Redevelopment angle: one-line thesis for the pathway.
  • Next steps: per-parcel actionable checks.
  • Action buttons: Google search, 🛰️ Aerial (Google Maps satellite search by the parcel's address — falls back to lat/lng if no address on file), and the correct county property-appraiser deep link.
  • Search listings: address-based search links for LoopNet / Crexi (commercial) and Zillow (residential/general). No free MLS API is accessible from a static frontend, so these open the parcel's address on each site — if a listing exists you see it in one click; if not, you get an empty-results page. Only surfaced when we have a physical address on file.
  • Est. max units: shown in the Value & density estimate box. Rough figure computed as acres × typical units/acre for the property type — see The Bill · §3 for the per-type ranges. Filter for this via Min est. units.
4 The watchlist

The watchlist is a lightweight shortlist stored in your browser's local storage. No account, no cloud, no sharing surface — it persists across sessions on this device only.

  • Add a parcel: click the ☆ icon on any card or in the detail drawer. Star fills in ★.
  • Add a note: with a parcel starred, a note input appears in the detail drawer. Notes save on every keystroke (debounced).
  • Filter to watchlist: click the Watchlist chip in the top bar. The map and list narrow to just your starred parcels. Click again to clear the filter.
  • Export: click Download Watchlist to grab a CSV of just your starred parcels (with your notes appended).
  • Remove: click the ★ icon again to unstar.

Where it lives: localStorage["sb1434.watchlist.v1"]. Clearing browser storage wipes it — copy anything important out to your own spreadsheet as insurance.

5 Tips by pathway

Golf courses (Pathways 1, 1B, 2)

Only Pathway 2 (not ringed) is unconstrained. For 1 and 1B the overlay caps density; if you own or optioning one, the play is repositioning (banquet, wellness, event space) rather than pure conversion.

Industrial (Pathway 3) & Auto/fuel (Pathway 9)

Assume contamination. Phase II ESA and a remediation estimate before LOI. Cleanup cost varies by 1-2 orders of magnitude between clean industrial and gas-station plumes.

Commercial (Pathway 4) & Office (Pathway 5)

Retail apocalypse plus post-COVID office vacancy. Verify tenant tails — a 10-year Wal-Mart NNN kills your teardown timing.

Residential redev (Pathway 7)

Mobile-home parks and aging garden apartments. Mind Florida tenant-displacement statutes — mobile-home park closures require 6+ months notice plus relocation assistance.

Vacant commercial (Pathway 11)

Least-encumbered starting point — no tenants to relocate. But vacant commercial in a brownfield zone often means the last owner walked away because remediation was uneconomic. Read the FDEP file before pursuing.

6 Filters & search reference
Search
Substring match on parcel ID or owner name (case-insensitive). When exactly one match is found, the map zooms to it. Press Escape to clear.
Property Type
Client-side filter on what the parcel actually is today (Golf Course, Warehouse, Shopping Center, etc.). Not the same as SB 1434 pathway — see §3 of the How It Works tab.
Min acres
Absolute minimum on acres. Defaults to 5 via Gate 1; raise it to strip out marginal parcels.
Min land/imp ratio
Minimum on the land/improvement ratio. Defaults to 1.0 on load, so the scanner starts by showing only land-heavy parcels — land worth as much or more than what's built. 999 = vacant / near-vacant. Clear the input to see everything.
Land-heavy only
Shortcut for min ratio ≥ 1.0 (redundant when the default is already 1.0, useful when you've raised the ratio above 1 and want to snap back).
Min est. units
Client-side minimum on the scanner's rough max-units estimate (acres × typical units/acre for the property type). Filter to "≥ 200 units" for meaningful-scale opportunities; "≥ 500" for large redevelopment plays. Estimates use property-type-typical densities documented in The Bill · §3 and are rough — the actual buildable count is set by local zoning, not the scanner.
Satellite
Swaps the map basemap.
Watchlist toggle
Restricts to your starred parcels.
Reset
Clears every filter + search + recenters the map. Watchlist stays intact.
Download CSV
Exports every currently-visible parcel with 23 columns (owner, address, JV, land ratio, coordinates, pathway, ring-test result, adjacency, env trigger). Filename sb1434_qualifying_parcels_YYYYMMDD.csv.
Download Watchlist
Same schema plus a note column. Filename sb1434_watchlist_YYYYMMDD.csv.
